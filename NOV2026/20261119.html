⭐ THEME

Prohibition of cutting flesh for the dead

📘 READING

“You shall not make any cuttings in your flesh for the dead, nor put tattoo marks on yourselves. I am YHVH.”
- Leviticus 19:28

“You are children of YHVH your Elohim. Do not cut yourselves nor shave the front of your head for the dead.”
- Deuteronomy 14:1

“For You are a set-apart people to YHVH your Elohim, and YHVH has chosen you to be a people for Himself, a treasured possession above all the peoples who are on the face of the earth.”
- Deuteronomy 14:2

🟫 MITSVOT FOCUS

The Torah prohibits cutting or marking our bodies as an expression of mourning for the dead. This command is not a denial of grief, nor does it require us to suppress the sorrow that comes when someone we love passes away. Rather, it establishes a boundary between genuine mourning and practices rooted in pagan beliefs about death, the spiritual world and the supposed power of the dead.

In the surrounding nations, bodily mutilation and ritual markings were sometimes used to express devotion to the deceased, seek their favour, or demonstrate allegiance to spiritual powers associated with death. YHVH commanded Yisra’el not to imitate these customs. His people were not to seek comfort, protection or spiritual connection through practices that dishonoured the body or departed from His instructions.

Our bodies are not ours to treat as objects for ritual expression. They are part of YHVH's creation and are entrusted to us. The prohibition therefore reminds us that even in sorrow, our response must remain within the boundaries of holiness and obedience.

Deuteronomy gives the reason clearly: we are children of YHVH and a set-apart people belonging to Him. Our identity does not change when we experience loss. We do not need to adopt the customs of the nations to demonstrate how deeply we loved someone, nor must we injure ourselves to prove the sincerity of our grief.

There is a difference between remembering the dead with love and participating in practices that attempt to maintain a spiritual relationship with them. We may honour someone's memory, speak about their life, preserve family history, visit their resting place and mourn their absence. None of these requires us to imitate forbidden rituals or seek guidance from the dead.

Yahusha HaMashiach also teaches us through His life that compassion and mourning are not signs of spiritual weakness. He wept with those who grieved, yet His ministry consistently directed people towards the living Elohim and the hope of life. Our grief may be real and profound, but our hope must remain anchored in YHVH.

Obedience in mourning means allowing ourselves to feel sorrow without surrendering our discernment. We can comfort one another, support grieving families and remember those who have passed away while remaining faithful to the Torah. We honour the dead appropriately by living in a manner that honours the One who gives life.

🩵 REFLECTION

When we experience the loss of someone dear to us, do we remember that our bodies and our lives belong to YHVH?

Are there mourning customs or inherited traditions that we have accepted without examining whether they agree with the Torah?

Do we distinguish between remembering someone with love and participating in practices that seek spiritual contact with the dead?

How can we support those who are grieving without encouraging customs that contradict our covenant responsibilities?

Our sorrow does not separate us from YHVH, and our obedience need not be abandoned in times of sorrow. We can grieve honestly, remember lovingly and continue walking in holiness. Our identity as His set-apart people remains unchanged.

👐 PRAYER

YHVH, our Elohim, thank You for giving us life and for teaching us how to walk in holiness even through the difficult experiences of life.

Help us to understand the boundaries You have established concerning mourning and the treatment of our bodies. Keep us from imitating practices that have their roots in pagan worship or attempts to communicate with the dead. Give us discernment to examine inherited customs against Your Torah rather than accepting traditions without question.

Comfort those who are grieving. Strengthen families who have experienced loss, and give them the support, patience and compassion they need. Teach us to stand beside those who mourn without judging their sorrow or encouraging practices that dishonour You.

Help us remember those who have passed away with gratitude for the lives they lived, while keeping our hope and trust firmly in You. May our conduct reflect our identity as Your set-apart people, and may we honour the gift of life by walking faithfully in Your instructions.

In the Name of Yahusha HaMashiach, our Redeemer and the hope of life. Emet.

Baruch YHVH. Emet.
